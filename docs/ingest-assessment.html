What the data actually means

A session is a folder: two video files, an audio file, a motion-sensor log, two calibration files, and a small JSON manifest describing it all. Collectors are paid per minute of usable footage, so one number in that folder decides what someone earns.

The manifest's duration_sec is the wrong number. It measures from the moment the device powers up to the moment it shuts down — start-up, shut-down and all. On session 072310 that is 12.852 s against 8.500 s of recorded video.

The subtler problem is that the streams do not start together. The motion sensor wakes 504 ms before the cameras and keeps running after they stop. So there are two plausible answers, and only one is honest:

Union versus intersection

Declared
12.852 s manifest
Union
9.286 s rejected
Intersection
8.500 s paid

The union — earliest start to latest end — is 9.286 s. It is always set by the motion sensor, because the motion sensor always starts first and ends last. Taking it would overpay by 9% on this session and by 25% on the equivalent glove-format recording we examined. The intersection is the window where every stream is recording, and it is what the engine reports.

Independent corroboration

Two unrelated sessions inflate by almost exactly the same factor: 1.51× on 072310 and 1.52× on 072516. That agreement is the strongest evidence the method is right rather than a coincidence of one recording.

Accuracy, honestly stated

Duration comes from one of four sources. Each is recorded on the episode, so nobody downstream has to guess how good a number is.

Timing sources, strongest first. Anything below exact raises a flag.
Source Confidence Resolution What limits it
pts_sidecar exact 1 µs One timestamp per frame, straight from the device clock. No estimation anywhere.
container derived ~1 frame The video knows its length but not when it started, so it can shorten the window and never move it.
imu_span estimated ~0.5 s The motion sensor brackets the cameras, so it always overstates. Used only when nothing else survives.
wall_clock estimated ~4 s Includes start-up and shut-down. Flagged for manual review rather than taken at face value.

Precision

Timestamps are read as BigInt, never floating point. A value like 1786605795008991 fits in a double today, but this number decides what people get paid and silent precision loss is exactly the bug that survives to production. They are stored in the record as decimal strings for the same reason — JSON has no integer type wide enough to be trusted with them.

The device clock is epoch microseconds on the same timebase as the manifest's wall clock, which we verified. That means declared and measured are directly comparable: a payment dispute is arithmetic, not interpretation.

A known inconsistency worth deciding on

The two strongest sources disagree by exactly one frame, systematically.

  • Sidecar reports first-timestamp to last-timestamp. For 256 frames that is 255 intervals.
  • Container reports the full presentation length. For the same frames that is 256 intervals.

Measured on 072538: format=duration gives 20.980044 s while the packet timestamps span 20.946667 s. The difference is 0.033377 s — one frame at 30 fps.

So a session timed from sidecars is reported about 33 ms shorter than the same session timed from its container. On an 8-second clip that is 0.4%; on an hour it is negligible. The specification defines raw duration as the timestamp span, so the engine follows it and reports 8.500 — but the two paths are not measuring quite the same thing, and settlement should know that before the figures reach a ledger.

Guardrails

Every rule below exists because the sample data broke something.

How a recording is dispositioned. Nothing is ever deleted.
StateMeaningEffect
ok Nothing found above informational. Enters review and settlement normally.
flagged Something a person should see: an unclosed session, a stream excluded, timing below exact. Still reviewed and still payable. Defects visible to the reviewer.
quarantined Cannot be trusted: calibration missing, media unreadable, a part missing from the middle. No review, no settlement, never deleted. Waits for a human.

Structural guarantees

  • The card is never written to. Not a lock file, not a marker, not a log. A test snapshots every filename, size and modification time before and after and asserts nothing moved. The TF card is evidence in a payment dispute.
  • Nothing is discarded. An empty folder, an unreadable video, a session with no manifest — all produce a record. The worst outcome is quarantine.
  • Same input, same output. Byte-identical apart from the timestamp of the run and the machine name. Re-running produces the same episode identity, so a session delivered twice by different routes resolves to one episode rather than two payments.
  • The manifest is a hint. It is read for context and recorded beside the measurements, and it decides nothing. A test deliberately corrupts its file list and asserts the output is unchanged.
  • Interrupted runs resume. Finished files are not re-hashed. The cache lives outside the source directory.

What the guardrails caught in real data

  • A motion-sensor log whose first 916 rows carry the epoch twice — 56 years off. Left unhandled it reported zero seconds for a session holding 10.4 s of good video. Underpaying, silently.
  • An audio timestamp file cut mid-digit at exactly 8192 bytes. Parsed naively the recording came out as minus 1.79 billion seconds.
  • A truncated video that still answers with a duration — and a longer one than the intact file: 8.515 s from a container missing 55% of its bytes. Counting packets exposes it, 121 against 256.
  • A statistics block copied verbatim from a different recording. Plausible numbers, wrong recording.

Can it ingest a video today?

Yes. It has, repeatedly, against real 17 MB and 44 MB recordings from the device. One command, one folder, one record.

Readiness

  • ReadyReads a real session folder end to end and emits a schema-validated record.
  • ReadyRuns offline. No network and no database in the core path, so it works at an upload centre with the link down.
  • Ready256 MB/s sustained, 225 MB of RAM on a 4.2 GB session. Faster than the upload it feeds.
  • ReadyMulti-part recordings, ordered by timestamp with the file numbering only as a tiebreak.
  • PartialThree of five sample sessions verified. The other two never survived the archive download and quarantine correctly as a result.
  • PartialEvery session with a manifest is flagged, because the camera-naming conflict fires on all of them. Until PaXini answers, that flag is noise a reviewer learns to ignore.
  • Not builtStoring episodes. The engine emits a record; something else has to keep it, and that is what would detect a checksum changing between deliveries.
  • Not builtUpload, proxy generation, review, settlement. All downstream consumers of this record, all out of scope.

Twenty synthetic sessions cover the failures the real data never produced: multi-part recordings, reversed part numbers, a hole in the middle of a sequence, a corrupt container, deleted calibration, a duplicate delivery, an absent manifest, unknown firmware. They are text-only and commit to the repository, so 23 of the 24 defect codes are provable in CI in five seconds with no sample data at all. The twenty-fourth needs a stored episode to compare against, which is the piece that has not been built.

What still needs a decision

  • Are the cameras colour or infrared? The manifest says color_left, the calibration says IR_L, and the calibration file contradicts itself internally — its ROS topic mentions color_left. This determines what the dataset is worth, and it currently flags every episode in the programme.
  • What stream skew is abnormal? 504 ms is observed and apparently normal, so the threshold is set at 1000 ms as a placeholder and has never fired.
  • Which firmware versions are in scope? Every sample is 1.0.3. Anything else flags, which is the safe default until the fleet is known.
  • The one-frame difference between the sidecar and container methods, described above. Small, systematic, and it should be a decision rather than an accident.
  • Re-download the sample archive. Two sessions are missing, including the 458 MB unclosed recording that is the best test of the "ingest it anyway" rule.
